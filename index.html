Florida B.E.S.T. · Grade 2 · Mathematics

Turn a FAST domain report into four days of the right practice.

FAST K–2 is an adaptive Star test. It gives you nine domain scores and no question-level detail at all — so the report tells you where a child is losing points but never why. This page closes that gap: enter the nine scores from your child’s Individual Student Report, and it maps each one to the exact Florida B.E.S.T. benchmarks behind it, the specific misconception most likely costing the marks, and a four-day plan that attacks the widest gap first.

It ships with 110 written practice items, procedural generators that produce unlimited fresh questions in every domain, an interactive 34-question mock built to the real test’s rules, and a review tab that shows only what went wrong.

The B.E.S.T. achievement scale

Diagnostic

Nine domains, ranked by how much room there is

A domain score is not "what he got wrong." The state defines it as an estimate: a score of 53 means the student "would be expected to correctly answer 53% of the grade-level items in that domain." On a 34-item adaptive test spread over nine domains he may have seen only two or three perimeter items, so treat each number as a signal with real error bars — but a 24 next to an 81 is a signal worth acting on.

The single most useful thing in this whole report

The state's own description of the domain he scored 24 on is narrow enough to quote in full: "Perimeter (Grade 2) assesses a student's understanding of perimeter by counting unit segments around the boundary of a polygon (without gaps or overlaps) and by using whole number side lengths." Despite the heading reading "Perimeter, Area, and Volume," there is no area and no volume on the grade 2 test. It is one skill. That is why Day 1 is entirely perimeter.

Ground rules

Running this with a child who finds sitting still hard

The math here is four days of work. The behavior is the actual project. Everything below is built around one idea: a child who refuses will fight you for control, so hand him control over everything that does not matter, and spend none on the thing that does. He never gets a choice about whether the block happens. He gets a choice inside every single one.

How to get 2½ hours out of a 7-year-old

Not in one block. That is the only rule that matters here. Two and a half hours is achievable in four days, but only as four separate sittings with real gaps between them — breakfast, then mid-morning, then after lunch, then late afternoon. Inside each sitting, work in 8–10 minute bursts with a movement break between. A child with a short attention span can sustain a great deal of math across a day; what he cannot do is sustain it continuously.

The second rule is that most of it is not at a desk. Each day is roughly 60 minutes on the screen and 85 minutes off it — walking taped shapes on the floor, hunting perimeters with string, measuring the house twice, running a coin shop. That is real practice and he can do hours of it, because it does not feel like a test. If you try to make 2½ hours of seated multiple choice happen, you will get one good day and three fights.

Don't
2½ hours in one sitting, or two 75-minute halves. By minute 25 you are no longer teaching math, you are managing behavior, and everything after that is negative.
Do
45 + 35 + 40 + 25, spread right across the day, with at least an hour of ordinary life between each. Same total, completely different child.

End every sitting while he is still winning, even if that means stopping at 20 minutes. A sitting that ends on a high note is worth three that end in a meltdown, and you have four days — you cannot afford to lose an evening to a fight.

Don't
"Do you want to do your math now?" — that is a yes/no question, and he will say no, and now you have a fight about compliance instead of a lesson.
Do
"Math is at 5. Do you want to start with perimeter or with the warm-up?" — the when is not negotiable, the order is his.

Six rules that do the heavy lifting

AUTONOMYTwo acceptable options at every decision point — which domain first, pen or pencil, kitchen table or floor, three questions or four. Never a yes/no. Never an open "what do you want to do?"
NO MID-FLOWDo not correct him while he is working. Interrupting a hyperactive child mid-thought reads as an attack and costs you the next ten minutes. Let the app mark it; it never argues back, and that is the point of putting a screen between you and the correction.
PRAISE EFFORT"You went back and checked that one" beats "you're so smart." Praise the behavior you want repeated — checking, re-reading, tracing the shape with a finger — not the ability.
MOVEMENT IS THE LESSONDo not treat the breaks as time off. Walk the perimeter of the rug while counting sides out loud. Hop a number line taped on the floor. Movement breaks measurably improve focus and behavior in exactly this profile, and here the movement is the math.
ONE WARNINGDecide the consequence before the session, state it once, calmly, and then follow through without a second warning. Repeated warnings teach a child who refuses that the boundary is negotiable.
TOKEN ECONOMYOne star per correct answer, banked in the app. Agreed exchange rate set before Day 1 and never renegotiated mid-session — renegotiating is the reward he is actually testing for. Token systems have good evidence as an add-on to everything else you are already doing.

The one rule that matters most: never end the block on a refusal

Evening-math refusal in a 7-year-old is almost always escape-motivated — the point of the behavior is to make math stop. So if refusing makes math stop, you have taught him, in about three goes, that refusing is the fastest way out. It will work for a week and then the refusals start at minute one. When he refuses, the demand does not disappear. It shrinks.

When he refuses — say this, once, flat

"You're doing two of these. Two, and we're done for tonight. You pick which two."

Two is a real win: he complied, the demand held, nobody fought. Tomorrow you ask for four. The only thing that ends a block early is you deciding it is over — announced before he refuses, or after he has finished something. Never straight after a refusal.

And give him a legal exit

A break he asks for is always granted, immediately, at no cost — that is what the Pause button in the app is for, and it is why the movement breaks do not run the clock. A child who has a legitimate way to escape does not need to manufacture one. Asking for a break is a skill you want to reinforce, not a loss of face.

Before you pick a time, answer two questions

Is he medicated, and when does the dose wear off? Late afternoon and early evening are when stimulant rebound usually lands, and rebound looks exactly like defiance — short fuse, tears, nothing is fair. If 5pm sits inside his rebound window you are not running a math session, you are running an experiment in frustration, and four evenings of it will change how you read your own child. Try a weekend morning or straight after school instead, and raise the timing with his prescriber before you conclude anything about his attitude. And feed him first: stimulants suppress appetite and he may have eaten very little all day.

Five more things, and they are the ones parents skip

WAIT TENGive the instruction once, standing close, as a statement. Then count to ten in your head and say nothing. Processing takes longer than it looks. Repeating inside that window is how you teach him the first instruction did not count. The moment he starts, name it: "You sat straight down. Thank you."
FOUR TO ONECount your own sentences for one session. If you corrected him more often than you caught him doing something right, the session was net punishing however calm you stayed. Aim at four positives per correction, and name the behavior — "you traced the whole edge with your finger", not "good job".
IGNORE THE NOISESighing, muttering, "this is stupid", sliding down the chair. If the pencil is still moving, he is complying. Say nothing about the noise, no eye contact, no "don't talk to me like that". Praise the math the instant he does the next one. Never ignored: anything unsafe, anything aimed at a person, anything broken.
WHEN, NOT IF"When the questions are done, then it's the tablet." Never "if". Same contingency, but "if" sounds like a bet he might lose and "when" sounds like a schedule.
SMALL AND FASTDecide the consequence in the afternoon, write it on a sticky note, and keep it small, immediate, related and over quickly — two minutes of the tablet, not a week. And never take back stars he has already earned. Confiscating banked rewards once teaches him nothing is safe and he will stop playing.

He is going to be right, and you have to say so

He will say "I already passed, this isn't fair." He is right. He met the standard, he is in the top 11% in the country, and he is doing extra math in the evening. Do not argue with a true statement — you will lose, and with a child who digs in the argument then becomes about honesty instead of math, which is a far worse fight. Agree first, then hold the line anyway, in one breath, and stop talking.

The version that works

"You're right. You did pass, and you did well, and you still have to do this. Both of those are true. Perimeter or the warm-up?"

You are half of this

When you notice your voice change, your jaw tighten, or that you are about to say something to win rather than to teach — you are done for the night, and that is a correct decision, not a failure. Have your own exit ready before you need it: "I'm getting some water. Two minutes." Never deliver a consequence while angry; you will over-deliver and then have to walk it back, which is worse than not delivering one. Hard stop: any session with two genuine blow-ups is over — "We're done, that's enough for tonight", no verdict attached, then something ordinary together for ten minutes. Missing a session costs almost nothing. Ending two days running on a fight costs you the whole plan.

Four days back to back — protect the schedule from one bad day

Consecutive-day intensity is how one bad session contaminates the next, so build the slack in deliberately. The first sitting of each day is the one that must happen; the fourth is the one you drop without comment if the day has gone badly. Losing a boss round costs almost nothing. Ending two days running on a fight costs you the whole plan.

And if the four days are driven by a school deadline you are still negotiating, remember what the report actually says: PM1 is informational, he already met the standard, and the attempt that counts is PM2 from 30 November. Four hard days now are worth doing — just do not let anyone tell him his September score was a failure.

Set the reward rate now, before Day 1

He earns 1 star for every question he attempts and 2 for every one he gets right, so a bad session still pays — which is what stops the spiral. Agree these three now and do not renegotiate mid-session; renegotiating is the reward he is actually testing for. He sees the bar and the next prize on his own screen the whole time.

Put these on the table before Day 1

Masking tape · graph paper · a ruler with inches and centimeters · unit cubes or square tiles · a coat hanger and clothes pegs · a handful of real coins (pennies, nickels, dimes, quarters) · a paper plate and a split pin for a clock · scratch paper and a pencil for every session · an index card with unit segment written on it, which he reads aloud once before the first question.

The plan

Four days, hardest gap first

Days are ordered by points available, not by what is easiest. Perimeter is day one because it is worth 76 points of headroom and is the narrowest skill on the whole test. Day 4 is the dress rehearsal.

Each day is four sittings, about 2½ hours in total, with a real gap between each one. The bar under each day shows the split: roughly an hour on the screen, the rest on the floor, round the house and in the kitchen. The app's drills will not run out — after the fixed 13 questions, Endless practice generates new shapes, rulers, clocks, coins and equations indefinitely, so a 35-minute drill never repeats itself.

Why there are two versions

FAST grades 3–10 run on a platform with back, next and flag. FAST K–2 runs on Renaissance Star, which locks every answer as final. Going back and adaptive testing are incompatible, so the practice test uses a fixed balanced form and lets him review and change answers, which is far better for learning; the real-rules test is adaptive and locked, for rehearsing the actual conditions. Do the practice one first.

Read this to him before the mock — it is what the proctor will say

Quoted from the K–2 FAST Test Administration Manual, which tells administrators to read it word for word. If he hears it four times this week it is familiar on the day instead of new — and notice how much of it is reassurance he needs anyway.

Read out loud

"Today you will take a test called Star. Some of the questions will be easy while others will be more challenging. Don't worry about how many questions you think you get right, or how many you think you get wrong. Everyone will likely miss some of the questions. The main goal is for you to do the best you can.

Be sure to look at all of the answer choices carefully before selecting your answer. If you do not know the answer to a question, go ahead and choose what you think is the best answer.

You will answer the questions on a computer or a tablet, but you can work the problems out on scratch paper.

Listen to the audio when a new question is presented to you. You will be able to pause, resume, or replay the audio if you need to.

Remember that once you have selected your answer, you will need to select Next to move on to the next question. You can change your answer choice before you select Next but not after. Once you move on to the next question you cannot go back.

If you see a picture of a clock at the top of the screen, time is almost up for that question. Choose your best answer and select Next to move on."

Two lines are worth saying twice: "everyone will likely miss some" and "go ahead and choose what you think is the best answer." A bright child who believes he must get everything right is the one who freezes on a hard item and loses the four after it.

Rehearse the thing that will actually cost him points

For a combined-type child the risk on this test is not knowledge, it is speed. The test cannot be skipped through and cannot be revisited, so a fast wrong answer is locked in forever, and the adaptive engine immediately hands him an easier question — which drags the estimate down twice. Practise one habit and only one: finger on the screen, read it twice, then choose. That is what the Read it to me button is for: if he cannot decode the question, the only two exits are a wild guess or a refusal, and both look like a math problem when they are not. Using it costs him nothing and is not a hint.
